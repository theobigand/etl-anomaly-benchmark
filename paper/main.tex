\begin{abstract}
Modern data pipelines increasingly depend on automated data quality
controls, yet Extract--Transform--Load (ETL) practitioners still rely
mostly on hand-crafted declarative rules. Recent work has started to
introduce machine learning (ML) anomaly detectors inside ETL workflows,
but systematic, apples-to-apples comparisons between rule-based,
classical ML, and deep learning approaches remain scarce---especially
when evaluated within a realistic pipeline and broken down by the type
of anomaly being injected. This paper presents an open-source ETL
pipeline in which anomaly detectors from different families share one
\texttt{fit}/\texttt{predict}/\texttt{score} interface and are scored on
the same pipeline path: three rule-based detectors (Z-score, IQR, Great
Expectations), eight classical ML detectors (Isolation Forest, LOF,
One-Class SVM, ECOD, COPOD, HBOS, KNN, PCA) and six neural ones
(autoencoder, VAE, Deep SVDD, DIF, AE1SVM, LUNAR), with an LSTM autoencoder
studied apart. I evaluate the
detectors, over five seeds on a stratified held-out split, on real open
datasets and on controlled synthetic ones. The real data is a pool of $152$ open
datasets, used as $100$ labelled problems and as $42$ tables into which I inject
anomalies; three of them are worked out as examples because they show three regimes:
Kaggle Credit Card Fraud, a rare-anomaly Cardio dataset and a
common-class Water Quality dataset. The synthetic data are generated with Faker, with
six distinct anomaly types injected at controlled rates. Beyond aggregate precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC and
PR-AUC, I report a per-anomaly-type recall and precision, precision-recall
curves, and a sweep of the injection severity. The aggregate metrics make the families look interchangeable;
the per-type view shows each has a narrow, largely non-overlapping
competence, so broad coverage needs several families rather than the
single best one. A larger registry does not change this: on the $100$ labelled
problems no detector is clearly ahead, the column-wise ECOD, COPOD and HBOS match
Isolation Forest, and the neural models do not beat the best classical one. My goal is a reproducible, pipeline-integrated
benchmark that answers the practical question: \emph{which family of
detectors should I use, for which kind of data quality problem, at what
operational cost?}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Extract--Transform--Load (ETL) pipelines are the backbone of modern data
infrastructure: they move raw operational data into analytical stores
where it feeds dashboards, reports, and machine-learning
applications. Organizations increasingly manage data as an asset that
must be governed~\cite{khatri2010data}, and the value delivered
downstream is bounded by the quality of the data that reaches the
destination. This is why data quality has long been a first-class
concern for data engineers, and why automated, continuous quality
control is a capability rather than an option.

The dominant approach in industry remains rule-based: practitioners
hand-write declarative expectations---``column \texttt{amount} must be
positive'', ``column \texttt{email} must match a regex''---in
frameworks such as Great Expectations, Deequ, or
Soda~\cite{dataqualitysurvey2024}. These rules are auditable and
cheap to run, but they scale poorly with schema complexity, detect only
violations the author anticipated, and miss subtle multi-column or
distributional anomalies. To address these limits, a recent line of
work has proposed to complement or replace such rules with machine
learning anomaly detectors embedded directly inside the ETL
flow~\cite{gopa2025aiaugmented,marupaka2024mlpredictive,sokkula2024implementing}.
The existing literature, however, suffers from three gaps.

First, the pipelines I reviewed are mostly presented as
architectures. Gopa~\cite{gopa2025aiaugmented} reports aggregate gains
taken from other implementations and releases no code, and the abstract
of Marupaka and Rangineni~\cite{marupaka2024mlpredictive} announces
accuracy gains without naming a dataset or a baseline. When an
experiment is reported, it exercises a single algorithm on a single
dataset: Sokkula and Vuppala~\cite{sokkula2024implementing} run
Isolation Forest on Credit Card Fraud after oversampling the training
data with SMOTE, and report an ROC-AUC of 0.51 with a fraud recall of
0.02. A near-random score on one dataset offers limited insight into
when ML-based quality control is actually worth adopting.

Second, the anomaly detection community has produced excellent
generic benchmarks---ADBench~\cite{han2022adbench} compares 30
algorithms on 57 tabular datasets; PyOD~\cite{zhao2019pyod} provides a
unified implementation of a wide range of detectors---but these benchmarks
evaluate algorithms on their own, outside of an ETL context. ADBench
reports training and inference times for each algorithm, but it has no
declarative rule-based baseline of the kind that dominates production
systems, and it does not measure what a detector costs inside a running
pipeline (latency, memory, operational overhead).

Third, benchmarks typically report a single aggregated score per
dataset, hiding the fact that different families of detectors excel at
different kinds of anomalies~\cite{goldstein2016comparative}. ADBench does look at anomaly types, but
as geometric types generated on synthetic data (local, global,
dependency and clustered anomalies), not as the data quality faults an
ETL pipeline meets. A Z-score detector is essentially unable
to flag categorical format violations; Great Expectations trivially
catches null-rate spikes but ignores multivariate
outliers; autoencoders learn joint
distributions but can be fooled by concept drift. A practitioner
choosing a detector today lacks a fine-grained, actionable map from
anomaly type to recommended method.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper addresses these three gaps. I
present:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A reproducible ETL pipeline} in which rule-based and
    classical ML anomaly detectors share one interface and are scored on
    the same extract--transform--detect path, released as open-source
    code. The registry holds three rule-based detectors, eight classical
    models and six neural ones, all behind the same interface.
    \item \textbf{A head-to-head benchmark} of statistical rules
    (Z-score, IQR, Great Expectations), classical ML detectors
    (Isolation Forest, LOF, One-Class SVM, ECOD, COPOD, HBOS, KNN, PCA) and neural
    detectors (autoencoder, VAE, Deep SVDD, DIF, AE1SVM, LUNAR) on real and synthetic datasets, with a
    stratified held-out split, five seeds, and the ML operating point
    read from the data, reporting precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC,
    precision-recall curves, and detection-stage latency (fit, predict and
    score; extraction, transformation and loading are not timed).
    \item \textbf{A per-anomaly-type analysis.} Using a synthetic
    dataset in which six distinct anomaly categories (missing values,
    duplicates, univariate outliers, multivariate outliers, temporal
    drift, invalid formats) are injected at a tunable severity, I produce
    a method-by-type recall and precision matrix, test its sensitivity
    under a severity sweep, add the neural models and the further classical detectors, and repeat it on two
    further schemas. The families turn out to have narrow, largely
    non-overlapping competence that the aggregate metrics hide.
    \item \textbf{The same analysis on real data.} The six types are
    injected into $42$ real tables of a pool of $152$ open datasets, and the
    detectors are also run on its $100$ labelled problems. The synthetic
    pattern holds only in part, and no detector is clearly ahead on the
    labelled problems.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~II reviews
related work on data quality in ETL and on anomaly detection
benchmarks. Section~III describes my pipeline architecture, the
detectors I compare, the datasets, the metrics and the verification of the
code. Section~IV reports the main results, on three example datasets taken from the
real-data pool, the synthetic generators and their variants, and the whole pool. Section~V
reports four secondary studies (a chronological split, decisions under
temporal shift, an LSTM autoencoder and the cost of detection). Section~VI discusses when rules, classical ML and
deep learning are each the right tool and what limits the evidence, and
Section~VII concludes. Appendix~A and B describe the verification of the
code and its outcomes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Data quality in ETL pipelines.}
Data quality is usually framed as governance: Khatri and
Brown~\cite{khatri2010data} treat data as an asset to be governed, and recent
surveys list the tools that check it, including Great
Expectations~\cite{greatexpectations}, Deequ and Soda among $17$
tools~\cite{dataqualitysurvey2024}, next to a broader survey of pipeline
tools~\cite{pipelinetoolssurvey2024}. These tools are rule-based. Deequ
expresses constraints as unit tests for data that run as aggregation queries on
Spark, supports incremental validation, and also suggests constraints and
detects anomalies in the time series of quality metrics~\cite{schelter2018automating}.
They check what the author of the rules thought of in advance; they do not say
how a learned detector would have done on the same data.

\paragraph{Anomaly detection algorithms.}
The detectors I compare are standard. Isolation
Forest~\cite{liu2008isolation}, LOF~\cite{breunig2000lof} and the One-Class
SVM~\cite{scholkopf2001support} are available in
PyOD~\cite{zhao2019pyod}, and Chandola et al.~\cite{chandola2009anomaly} survey
the field. Goldstein and Uchida~\cite{goldstein2016comparative} compare $19$
unsupervised algorithms on $10$ datasets and find that the choice depends on
the type of anomaly, local or global, which is the observation this paper takes
to data quality faults. For reconstruction models, Malhotra et
al.~\cite{malhotra2016lstm} train an LSTM encoder-decoder on normal behaviour
and flag high reconstruction error, on public time series (power demand, space
shuttle, ECG) and on engine sensors; my LSTM autoencoder follows that idea on windows of tabular rows.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
ADBench~\cite{han2022adbench} is the closest work: $30$ algorithms on $57$
datasets, $98{,}436$ experiments, with different levels of supervision and four
types of anomalies (local, global, dependency and clustered) generated from
existing data, reported with AUCROC and AUCPR and with training and inference
time. In its text I found no threshold-dependent metric and no chronological
protocol, and none of its anomaly types is a data-quality fault such as a
missing value or an invalid format. Agyemang~\cite{agyemang2024anomaly}
compares five unsupervised detectors on one simulated dataset with accuracy,
precision, recall and $F_1$, and leaves real data as future work (I read the
abstract only). AnoShift~\cite{dragoi2022anoshift} builds a time-ordered
protocol on ten years of Kyoto-2006+ network traffic, training on 2006--2010 and
testing on 2011--2013 (NEAR) and 2014--2015 (FAR), and reports ROC-AUC and PR-AUC
for classical and deep models, again without a decision threshold.
FedAD-Bench~\cite{anwar2024fedadbench} compares deep models in a federated
setting on four tabular datasets and chooses each threshold by maximising $F_1$
on a validation set with the anomaly ratio of the test set. Sun et
al.~\cite{sun2024online} propose online thresholds with confidence sequences
that adapt to distribution shift, and test them on synthetic and real data. They
study how to pick a threshold, not how classical detectors behave in a pipeline.

\paragraph{Machine learning in ETL pipelines.}
Papers on machine learning inside ETL
mostly describe architectures. Gopa~\cite{gopa2025aiaugmented} reports aggregate
figures taken from enterprise deployments without code, and
Marupaka and Rangineni~\cite{marupaka2024mlpredictive} announce accuracy gains in
an abstract that names no dataset or baseline. Sokkula and
Vuppala~\cite{sokkula2024implementing} run an Isolation Forest on the
resampled Credit Card data and obtain an AUC-ROC of $0.51$.

\paragraph{Gap and what is not claimed.}
Among these sources I did not find a comparison of rule-based, classical and
learned detectors that reports their decisions, not only a ranking, broken
down by the kind of data-quality fault, on both a controlled generator and
real tables, and in the setting of an ETL pipeline with its cost. That is the
gap this paper addresses, with the limits stated in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
I do not claim that no such study exists: the scan covers the works above, was
not systematic, and I read the full text of only some of them. I do not claim
that the detectors are new, that the injected faults match natural ones, or that
the results hold beyond the datasets used.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Pipeline}
Detectors are evaluated inside a minimal Extract--Transform--Load
pipeline rather than in isolation. The extract stage reads the raw table,
the transform stage removes exact duplicate rows, and the quality stage
applies a detector that assigns each row a boolean anomaly flag and a
continuous anomaly score. All detectors share a common
\texttt{fit}/\texttt{predict}/\texttt{score} interface modelled on
PyOD~\cite{zhao2019pyod}, so that classical and deep models can later be
dropped in without changing the harness.

\subsection{Rule-based detectors}
This stage compares three rule-based baselines. All three define a band of
``normal'' values per column and flag a row when it leaves the band on at
least one column; they differ in how the band is estimated and in how
robust that estimate is to the very outliers being sought.

\emph{Z-score} standardizes each numeric column on the fitted data and
flags a row when any column exceeds $|z| > 3$. The threshold follows the
empirical rule: under an approximately normal distribution about $99.7\%$
of values lie within $3\sigma$, so beyond it is rare by construction. Its
weakness is that the mean and standard deviation are themselves inflated
by extreme values, so a large outlier can mask others.

\emph{IQR} learns Tukey's fences $[Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR},\,
Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}]$ per column, where the interquartile range spans
the central $50\%$ of the data, and flags a row falling outside any fence.
Quartiles are robust: unlike the mean and standard deviation they barely
move when extreme values are added, so the fences are not distorted by the
outliers they are meant to catch. The $1.5$ multiplier corresponds to
about $2.7\sigma$ under normality, isolating a comparable tail.

\emph{Great Expectations} encodes a declarative expectation
suite~\cite{dataqualitysurvey2024} and makes no distributional
assumption: a not-null expectation on every column and a range expectation
per numeric column, with bounds set to the $0.1$th and $99.9$th
percentiles of the fitted data. Using inner percentiles rather than the
observed min/max is what turns the range check into a usable rule instead
of a no-op on the fitting data. A row is flagged when it violates at least
one expectation; its score is the fraction of the suite it violates.

The continuous scores of the statistical detectors are the worst
standardized deviation (Z-score) and the largest fence exceedance in IQR
units (IQR). The axis of difference between the three is thus robustness
and the assumption made about the shape of the data, which is precisely
what the learned models of the next stage are meant to handle better.

\subsection{Classical machine-learning detectors}
The second family replaces the per-column band with a learned model of
the data, so that a row can be flagged for an implausible
\emph{combination} of otherwise-normal values. Eight unsupervised
detectors are taken from PyOD~\cite{zhao2019pyod} rather than
reimplemented, with two deviations explained below. All receive the
same preparation: non-numeric columns are dropped, missing cells are
median-imputed, and every feature is standardized, since several of them
are distance-based. The PyOD \texttt{decision\_function} provides the
continuous score; the boolean flag uses a \emph{contamination} equal to
the anomaly prevalence of the training split (clamped to PyOD's valid
range), so each model marks about as many rows as the data actually
contains rather than a fixed share. Contamination sets only the flag
threshold and leaves ROC-AUC and PR-AUC unaffected. All hyperparameters
are the defaults of PyOD; I tuned none of them.

Three of the eight are the usual references. \emph{Isolation
Forest}~\cite{liu2008isolation} scores a row by how few random
axis-aligned splits are needed to isolate it; \emph{Local Outlier
Factor} (LOF)~\cite{breunig2000lof} compares a point's local density to
that of its neighbours; and a \emph{One-Class
SVM}~\cite{scholkopf2001support} fits a boundary enclosing the bulk of
the training data.

Five more complete the family, and they differ mainly in whether they
look at the columns one by one or together. Three of them model each
column on its own and add up the evidence, so they can flag an unusual
value in a column but not an unusual pair of values.
\emph{ECOD}~\cite{li2023ecod} estimates, for each column, the share of
fitted rows below a value and the share above it (the empirical tail
probabilities); a row scores the sum over columns of the negative
logarithm of the smaller tail, the tail being chosen by the sign of the
skewness of the column. \emph{COPOD}~\cite{li2020copod} uses the same
tail probabilities but combines the two tails of a column by their mean,
which is an empirical copula without dependence between columns.
\emph{HBOS}~\cite{goldstein2012hbos} builds a histogram of each column and
scores a row by the sum of the logarithms of the inverse heights of the
bins its values fall in. The two others use the joint distribution.
\emph{KNN}~\cite{ramaswamy2000efficient} scores a row by its distance to
its fifth nearest neighbour among the fitted rows, the simplest
distance-based detector and the one LOF refines. \emph{PCA}
projects the rows onto the principal components that explain $95\%$ of
the variance and scores the squared reconstruction error: a row that
follows the correlations of the fitted data is rebuilt almost exactly,
one that breaks them is not, while a shift that stays inside the kept
components is invisible to it. The idea of detecting outliers with
principal components is that of Shyu et al.~\cite{shyu2006principal}.

Two choices differ from what PyOD does by default, because a detector in my
pipeline must map a row to a score using the fitted rows only. PyOD
recomputes the empirical distribution of ECOD and COPOD on the fitted
rows plus the rows being scored, so a row's score depends on the other
rows evaluated with it. I recompute the two methods on the fitted rows
alone, ranking a row as if it were added to them, which is what PyOD
computes for a batch of one row (a test compares the two). PyOD's own PCA
scores a weighted distance to the eigenvectors, not a reconstruction
error, so the projection comes from scikit-learn. LOF and KNN compare every
row with the fitted rows, and the One-Class SVM is quadratic too; they are
skipped, and the skip recorded, above $50{,}000$ fitted rows for LOF and KNN and
$20{,}000$ for the One-Class SVM (limits set from the cost study).

\subsection{Deep detectors}
The third family learns a representation with a neural network. They are
opt-in in the registry, they need PyTorch, and they run on the CPU. Six
models are compared, all behind the same interface and all with the default
settings of PyOD~2~\cite{chen2025pyod2}.
The \emph{autoencoder} is a multilayer perceptron (scikit-learn) trained to
rebuild the standardized features through a bottleneck; a row that breaks the
structure learned on the fitted data is rebuilt badly, and the squared
reconstruction error is its score. The \emph{variational autoencoder}
(VAE)~\cite{kingma2014auto} encodes a row into a Gaussian code and decodes
it, and is trained on a bound of the likelihood of the data; its score is the
reconstruction error. PyOD draws the code at random when it scores, so a
row's score changes from one call to the next and with the rows around it;
I score with the mean of the code instead. \emph{Deep SVDD}~\cite{ruff2018deep}
trains a network to map the fitted rows close to a fixed centre and scores a
row by its distance to that centre. The \emph{Deep Isolation
Forest} (DIF)~\cite{xu2023deep} maps the rows through an ensemble of randomly
initialized networks and builds an isolation forest in each representation;
PyOD standardizes the representation of the scored rows with their own mean
and variance, which makes a score depend on the other scored rows, so I
standardize it with the fitted rows. \emph{AE1SVM}~\cite{nguyen2019scalable}
trains an autoencoder and a one-class SVM approximated with random Fourier
features on its code, and scores the combination. \emph{LUNAR}~\cite{goodge2022lunar}
learns the score from the distances of a row to its nearest neighbours, which
generalizes LOF and KNN with a network. The neural models are fitted on at
most $20{,}000$ randomly drawn fitted rows to bound the duration, and each is
seeded with the seed of the repetition. The LSTM autoencoder of
Section~\ref{sec:lstm} reads windows of ordered rows, does not share the
row-level contract, and stays outside this registry.

\subsection{Synthetic per-anomaly-type dataset}
\label{sec:synthetic}
The real datasets carry a single binary label that mixes every kind of
defect together, so they cannot say \emph{which} problem a detector is
good at. I therefore generate a controlled dataset of $12{,}360$
customer records with Faker and NumPy, in which income is correlated with
balance and transaction size and account age is spread over three years.
Into this clean population I inject six anomaly types, each at a rate of
$3\%$ and each row tagged with its category: \emph{missing values} (one
or two cells nulled), \emph{exact duplicates} (a clean row copied),
\emph{univariate outliers} (one numeric field pushed far out of range),
\emph{multivariate outliers} (every field individually in range but the
combination implausible, e.g.\ an $18$-year-old with a top-percentile
balance and income), \emph{temporal drift} (recent-signup rows whose
income and balance distribution has shifted upward by half), and
\emph{invalid formats} (malformed e-mail, out-of-domain country code,
impossible age). Because the category of every injected row is known, I
report, per method and per type, both the detection rate (recall) and the
precision on a stream made of clean rows plus that one type. The injection
magnitudes are exposed as a single \emph{severity} multiplier so the
choice of magnitude can be swept rather than fixed
(Section~\ref{sec:severity}). I also add a time-aware detector,
\emph{drift}, that learns a per-feature baseline from the oldest rows and
flags recent rows that both sit in a window whose mean has moved and lean
in the direction of that move; it is run on the synthetic dataset only, as
the other detectors ignore the row order.

\subsection{Datasets}
The real data is one pool of $152$ open datasets from ADBench and the UCI repository
(open licence, pinned by checksum; \texttt{verification/dataset\_pool.md}). I use it in two
ways: $100$ of its problems already carry labelled anomalies and are run as they are
(Section~\ref{sec:labelled}), and $42$ of its tables are large enough ($3{,}000$ rows, four
continuous columns) to receive injected anomalies (Section~\ref{sec:typed}). Three datasets
are worked out in detail as examples, because they illustrate three regimes of the method.
\emph{Credit Card Fraud}~\cite{mlgulbcredit}
(Kaggle, also one of the ADBench problems;
$283{,}726$ transactions after de-duplication, $30$ numeric features,
$0.17\%$ labelled fraud) is the standard strongly imbalanced benchmark,
but its positive class mixes every kind of defect.
\emph{Cardio} (a cardiotocography anomaly dataset as packaged for
outlier-detection benchmarking, another ADBench problem; $1{,}831$ rows, $21$ standardized
features, $9.6\%$ anomalies) is a \emph{genuinely imbalanced} real
dataset~\cite{han2022adbench,campos2000cardio}: the anomalies are rare and distributionally
distinct, the regime the unsupervised detectors are built for.
\emph{Water Quality}~\cite{kadiwal2021water} (a Kaggle dataset of the same kind, outside the pool;
$3{,}276$ samples,
$9$ numeric features) is the opposite case: the target class
(``not potable'') is common ($61\%$) rather than rare, and the data has genuine missing values,
so it probes the detectors outside their regime.
Beside the real data, a \emph{synthetic} dataset generated with Faker
($12{,}360$ rows, $17.5\%$ anomalous, described above) carries the per-type
labels, and two further generators repeat it on other schemas
(Section~\ref{sec:schemas}), to test particular cases in controlled numbers.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Each dataset is split $70/15/15$ (train/validation/test), stratified on
the label. For the synthetic dataset, rows are grouped by customer identity
before stratification so an original and its exact copy cannot cross
partitions. Detectors are fitted on the train split and every reported
number is computed on the held-out test split; the validation split is
reserved for later tuning. The ML contamination is read from the train
split's prevalence. This is a label-informed operating-point choice,
although estimator fitting itself does not use the labels. The real-data
benchmark also permits a contamination value specified before evaluation,
independent of every split's labels. This label-free preset forms a distinct
comparison protocol; the numerical results in this manuscript retain the
train-prevalence baseline. In the sensitivity sweep, contamination follows a
fixed grid and no value is selected from the test metrics. Threshold or
hyperparameter selection for a later confirmatory experiment would use
training or validation data only. The whole
procedure is repeated over five random
seeds and I report the mean and standard deviation. This spread measures
the sensitivity to the split and to model randomness on a fixed dataset; it
is not a confidence interval for performance on new data. LOF and the
One-Class SVM are run on Water Quality, Cardio and the synthetic dataset
but not on Credit Card Fraud, where LOF's superlinear scoring and the
One-Class SVM's quadratic training were expected to be too costly at
$284$k rows; this was not measured.

Because the detectors emit both a threshold decision and a graded score,
I report threshold metrics (precision, recall, $F_1$) alongside
ROC-AUC and PR-AUC (computed as average precision, not trapezoidal
area). On imbalanced data PR-AUC is the more informative
metric: the false-positive rate underlying ROC-AUC is diluted by the large
true-negative pool and stays optimistic, whereas precision reacts directly
to false alarms. I keep both because PR-AUC's random baseline equals the
prevalence and is not comparable across datasets, while ROC-AUC's is
always $0.5$ and is. I also report the fraction of rows flagged and the
in-pipeline latency. Because a single flag threshold hides the rest of the
trade-off, I additionally draw the full precision-recall curve of every
detector (Fig.~\ref{fig:prcurves}) and sweep Isolation Forest's
contamination across each dataset (Table~\ref{tab:contamination}). The
deep-learning detectors (Autoencoder, LSTM-Autoencoder) are the object of
the next stage.

\subsection{Verification and reproducibility}
\label{sec:verification}
The code that produces the results is checked at several levels, described in
Appendix~\ref{app:design}. A routine suite exercises numerical contracts on small
generated tables; a mutation campaign and twenty-three deliberate scientific
defects ask whether those tests can fail; a real-data check compares the
three research files with an archived manifest; and a release script turns the
evidence into a verdict (\emph{certified}, \emph{incomplete} or \emph{rejected}) tied to the
source revision and to a fingerprint of the code that computes results. The
verdict states where the evidence was produced and never that it was
independently attested: I wrote the code and launched every run. A failed or
missing check is a limitation of the evidence, not a successful experiment. The
measured outcomes are in Appendix~\ref{app:outcomes}. The chronological split and
the window-based drift monitor have a separate protocol: their alert and delay
measures are not pooled with the row-level metrics of the stratified benchmark.

\subsection{Example 1: Credit Card Fraud}
Table~\ref{tab:fraud} reports the detectors on Credit Card Fraud: the
eight that scale to $284$k rows, then the six neural ones, which are fitted on

\subsection{Synthetic dataset: by anomaly type}
\label{sec:results-synthetic}
Fig.~\ref{fig:heatmap} gives the mean recall of each method of the registry on each
injected type, with the LSTM autoencoder of Section~\ref{sec:lstm} for reference. The structure
the aggregate table hides is now clear: most methods have a narrow competence, and the
neural models and the new classical ones change the picture only on some columns.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Univariate outliers} are caught by every distributional
    method (recall $0.68$--$1.00$) except Deep SVDD ($0.33$), and only by those. Z-score's precision
    on them is $1.00$: it never fires on a clean row.
    \item \emph{Missing values} are caught best by the Great Expectations
    not-null expectations (recall $1.00$, precision $0.94$), because the
    statistical rules propagate \texttt{NaN} to a non-flag and the learned
    models see imputed values. LUNAR ($0.44$) and LOF ($0.28$) are the next best,
    and every other model stays below $0.20$.
    \item \emph{Exact duplicates} are nearly invisible to every detector
    (recall at most $0.18$, except LUNAR with $0.39$). The
    deduplication rule of the transform stage is the only thing that
    catches them, at precision $0.50$ because it also flags each row's
    clean original. In this grouped split the rule is evaluated within
    the held-out partition; it is a stream-level reference, not a model.
    \item \emph{Multivariate outliers} separate the families, and here the new detectors
    matter. Isolation Forest ($0.89$) and the One-Class SVM ($0.90$) were the only
    methods with real recall among the first six, since they are the only ones that
    model the joint distribution. Among the neural models, DIF ($1.00$), the VAE
    ($0.92$), the autoencoder ($0.83$) and AE1SVM ($0.78$) do as well, and LUNAR ($0.63$)
    follows; Deep SVDD ($0.13$) does not. KNN ($0.39$) and PCA ($0.48$) are below the
    forest. ECOD and COPOD, which look at each column alone, still reach $0.52$ and
    $0.51$, which I had not expected; probably the injected combination also puts one of the
    values in a tail of its own column. HBOS, built on histograms of single columns,
    stays at $0$. The precision of Isolation Forest and the One-Class SVM on this
    type is only about $0.22$: they cast a wide net that also catches clean rows.
    \item \emph{Invalid formats} are caught mainly through the impossible
    numeric value they carry (IQR recall $1.00$, precision $0.37$; Z-score
    precision $1.00$; DIF $1.00$, LUNAR $0.90$, KNN $0.81$); the malformed string fields
    are missed by all.
    \item \emph{Temporal drift} is the weakest column for most methods. LUNAR is the best
    ($0.65$), ahead of LOF ($0.50$), the best of the first six; every other method stays
    below $0.42$. The dedicated \emph{drift} detector scores
    $0$ here too: at the reference magnitude the shifted values stay
    inside the global range, and once diluted by the many clean recent
    rows the window mean has moved by less than a tenth of a standard
    deviation on average ($0.04$ to $0.11$ over the five test splits). The
    present row-level drift detector remains at zero even in the severity
    sweep; the windowed-monitoring extension is separate.
\end{itemize}
The practical reading is a short decision list: null-rate problems to
declarative rules, single-column outliers to any statistical rule,
implausible combinations to a tree-, boundary- or reconstruction-based model
(Isolation Forest, the One-Class SVM, DIF, the VAE), and
duplicates and drift to dedicated stages the detectors do not replace, with LUNAR as the
one learned model that helps on drift at the price of more false alarms (Section~\ref{sec:typed}).

\subsection{Detection rate versus injection severity}
\label{sec:severity}
The synthetic magnitudes are a design choice, so I sweep them. A single
\emph{severity} multiplier scales the univariate factor, the multivariate
percentile extremity and the temporal-drift multiplier ($1.0$ is the
dataset used above). Fig.~\ref{fig:severity} plots per-type recall against
severity. Univariate outliers are already saturated at severity $0.5$.
Multivariate outliers show a threshold: Isolation Forest and the
One-Class SVM climb to recall $1.0$, while IQR jumps from $0.04$
to $1.0$ once the injected percentile crosses its fence. Temporal drift
becomes easier for IQR, Isolation Forest and One-Class SVM (roughly
$0.2$--$0.3$ recall at severity $1.0$ and $0.6$--$0.7$ at severity $3$),
while LOF peaks earlier and then falls. The row-level drift detector
does not respond in this sweep. The ranking therefore changes with
severity, limiting claims based on one injection magnitude.

\subsection{Typed injection on real datasets}
\label{sec:typed}
The synthetic matrix of Section~\ref{sec:results-synthetic} comes from a single
generator. To see whether its pattern depends on that generator, the six
anomaly types were injected into $42$ real tables from the UCI repository
(open licence, pinned by checksum; \texttt{verification/dataset\_pool.md}). For
each table only the continuous columns are kept, and for labelled sets only the
normal class, so that the original anomalies never enter the evaluation. The
injection follows the synthetic one (three percent of rows per type, same
split, seeds and detector settings), with numeric versions of the types: a
value several standard deviations away, a pair of correlated columns pushed to
opposite tails, a shift of three columns for the most recent rows, a sign
flip, a sentinel or a thousandfold scale error. The protocol and five
hypotheses were written before any run
(\texttt{verification/typed\_injection.md}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/typed_pooled.png}
\caption{Median detection rate over $42$ real datasets with typed injection.
Compare with Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}.}
\label{fig:typed}
\end{figure}

Two rows of the synthetic pattern hold on every table: Great Expectations finds
all missing values and the deduplication rule all duplicates. The rest does not
carry over. Three of the five hypotheses are not supported (Great Expectations
and deduplication aside, the specialisation, the multivariate gap and the
difficulty of drift). On multivariate outliers the median detection rate is
$0.37$ for Isolation Forest and $0.17$ for the one-class SVM, against $0.89$ and
$0.90$ on the synthetic data. The interquartile fences reach the best detection
rate on several types, but they also flag a median of $22\%$ of the clean rows
(up to $99\%$ on some wide tables), so the detection rate alone favours them.
The $z$-score detects almost nothing on univariate outliers (median $0.00$
against $0.93$ synthetically). A check made after seeing this, and therefore
exploratory, shows why: the invalid rows of the training split inflate the
standard deviation, and without them the $z$-score detects every univariate
outlier on all $42$ tables. The result says more about non-robust statistics
on a contaminated training set than about the $z$-score itself.

The two hypotheses on stability hold. The mean rank correlation between the
method-by-type matrices of two tables is $0.80$ with the seventeen detectors of the
registry (it was $0.83$ with the first eight rows), and it is lowest for drift
($0.63$), multivariate outliers ($0.65$) and duplicates ($0.66$), highest for univariate
outliers ($0.76$). Part of this stability is mechanical, since
many cells are close to $0$ or $1$. The detection rate of Isolation Forest on
univariate outliers falls with the number of continuous columns (rank
correlation $-0.96$ over the $42$ tables). The anomalies are injected, not
natural, and at one severity.

The classical and neural detectors added to the registry were run on the same $42$
tables (Figure~\ref{fig:typed}), without a hypothesis of their own on this study. On
multivariate outliers LUNAR has the highest median detection rate ($0.63$, Isolation Forest
$0.37$, KNN $0.30$, the autoencoder $0.29$, DIF $0.28$, ECOD $0.25$), and also on drift
($0.78$ against $0.64$ for the IQR rule and $0.45$ for Isolation Forest) and on missing
values among the learned models ($0.38$, Great Expectations $1.00$). It pays for it: it flags a
median of $24\%$ of the clean rows ($97\%$ on the worst table), where the other learned
models, calibrated to the same training prevalence, flag $12\%$ to $15\%$ and HBOS $1\%$.
Only the IQR rule and LUNAR flag more than a fifth of the clean rows on median. The
column-wise ECOD and COPOD detect univariate outliers less often than KNN ($0.44$ and
$0.45$ against $0.91$) and HBOS almost never ($0.04$). So the synthetic reading holds in outline:
no detector covers every type, and the one that comes closest buys its recall with false alarms.

\subsection{Labelled problems of the dataset pool}
\label{sec:labelled}
The three examples above are only a few datasets. The pool of $152$ open
tables (\texttt{verification/dataset\_pool.md}), of which Credit Card Fraud and Cardio are members, holds $100$ problems whose
anomalies are already labelled: $36$ from ADBench and $64$ from the UCI
repository, where the rarest class (at least $20$ rows) is the anomaly and the

\subsection{Neural models and further classical detectors in the per-type matrix}
\label{sec:deep-rows}
The first six rows of Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} have no learned reconstruction model. I
added two on the same synthetic data and the same identity-grouped splits
and seeds, with the protocol and four hypotheses written beforehand
(\texttt{verification/deep\_typed.md}): the sklearn autoencoder of the registry,
and the LSTM autoencoder of Section~\ref{sec:lstm} on windows of the ten
preceding customers ordered by signup date. Unlike the Credit Card pilot, both
models were trained until their validation loss stopped improving (patience of
$5$, at most $300$ epochs), and all ten trainings stopped before the cap
($60$ to $178$ epochs). The other rows were recomputed by the same code and
equal the published matrix.

Those two rows do not change the picture. The autoencoder detects multivariate
outliers ($0.83 \pm 0.10$) about as well as Isolation Forest ($0.89$) and
One-Class SVM ($0.90$), as expected for a model that reconstructs correlations,
and the LSTM is a little lower ($0.68 \pm 0.12$). Both are close to the learned
detectors on the structural types, and on missing values and duplicates they are
no better than the best row-level detector. The hypothesis I expected to
matter, that the sequence model would catch temporal drift better than every
row-level detector, is not supported: its recall is $0.25 \pm 0.07$ against
$0.50$ for LOF, $0.33$ for Isolation Forest and $0.29$ for the autoencoder.
Three of the four hypotheses hold (structural types, convergence, multivariate
outliers). The synthetic customers are independent rows, so the signup order
carries a sequence only for the drifted rows, which are the most recent ones; a
window of ten rows that are mostly clean and have shifted values in only some of
the columns gives the LSTM little to learn from. This says that this
construction of drift is not a sequence problem, not that sequence models
cannot detect drift. With five seeds the standard deviations are indicative and none of
these comparisons is a test.

The complete-registry study then added five classical detectors and five neural ones
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) to the same matrix, with three hypotheses on it written
beforehand (\texttt{verification/full\_registry.md}). None is supported. The
detectors that read one column at a time were expected to miss multivariate outliers (recall
at most $0.20$ for ECOD, COPOD and HBOS, and Isolation Forest at least $0.50$ above them): only HBOS does
($0.00$), while ECOD and COPOD reach $0.52$ and $0.51$. PCA was expected to match the
autoencoder within ten points, since both rebuild the rows, and it does not ($0.48$ against
$0.83$): a reconstruction through $95\%$ of the variance rebuilds a row that breaks the
correlations only when the broken direction is a minor one. No new detector was expected to
beat the best earlier row-level detector on drift by more than ten points; LUNAR does
($0.65$ against $0.50$ for LOF). Its other rows are also the highest of the matrix
on missing values ($0.44$) and duplicates ($0.39$), but it flags $41\%$ of the clean rows, against
$9\%$ to $16\%$ for the other neural models, so its recall comes with a much higher cost in false
alarms; the same holds on the real tables of Section~\ref{sec:typed}. DIF reaches $1.00$ on both univariate and
multivariate outliers and on invalid formats, with $9\%$ of clean rows flagged; the VAE ($0.92$)
and AE1SVM ($0.78$) follow the autoencoder on multivariate outliers; Deep SVDD is the weakest
neural model on univariate and multivariate outliers, drift and invalid formats.

\section{Secondary Studies}
\label{sec:secondary}
The four studies below are narrower than the per-type comparison of the
previous section. Each uses one or two datasets or a synthetic stream, was
written down before its run, and answers one question: whether a chronological
split changes the ranking, whether a threshold chosen on the past survives a
shift in prevalence, whether a sequence model adds anything, and what detection
costs inside the pipeline. They are reported separately so that the main result does
not rest on them.

\subsection{Chronological pilot on Credit Card}
\label{sec:chrono}
The results above use a random stratified split. Of the three example datasets,
only Credit Card has a time variable (seconds since the first transaction,
about two days); Cardio and Water Quality have none, so their row order says
nothing about time. I therefore ran one small pilot on Credit Card, with the
protocol written down before any score was computed on held-out data
(\texttt{verification/temporal\_pilot.md}). It is a separate experiment and
does not replace the benchmark.

The rows are the same cleaned rows, and the detectors are the four of the

chronological split uses the usual $70/15/15$ ratios by time: the detectors

of magnitude, not a general rule.

The four detectors added later behave differently from one another. ECOD and COPOD
lose part of their stratified average precision ($0.175$ to $0.075$ and $0.212$ to $0.127$)
and, like Isolation Forest, flag under $0.1\%$ of the future rows; they find one
fraud and three of the $52$ (recall $0.02$ and $0.06$). HBOS keeps the highest
chronological average precision of the table ($0.151$, interval
$[0.058, 0.268]$ overlapping the others'), with a precision of $0.26$ and a recall of $0.19$ on
$0.09\%$ of the rows: the first detector of this study whose training-prevalence threshold
still catches frauds in the later period. PCA collapses ($0.002$, ROC-AUC $0.61$), with
no fraud found. The intervals are wide, so this shows that the column-wise
detectors are not all alike under a time split, not that HBOS is better.

To check the window monitor with a known change, a mean shift of $\delta$
training standard deviations was added to five features from the middle of the
test period. The alert threshold was fixed from the validation period before
the test (the largest validation window score, $0.91$). Shifts of $1$ and $2$
standard deviations were detected in the second window after the change
($573$ seconds), and a shift of $0.5$ was not detected; no window before the
change raised an alert ($22$ windows). Credit Card has no labelled change in
distribution, so I make no claim about its natural drift.

\subsection{Decisions under temporal shift}
\label{sec:decisions}
The pilot above leaves two questions open: whether a threshold chosen on the past
can be repaired without labels, and how much of the gap is only a change in
prevalence. I ran a second study on Credit Card and on Bank Marketing, a UCI
table (CC BY 4.0) whose file is ordered by date from May 2008 to November 2010,
with the protocol and four hypotheses written beforehand
(\texttt{verification/temporal\_decision.md}). The Credit Card results were
already known, so this study is an independent test only for Bank Marketing. The
Kyoto-2006+ traffic used by AnoShift was left out, because I could not verify
its data rights nor download it in time. The date of Bank Marketing is a month,
with the file order inside each month. Four detectors (Z-score, IQR, Great
Expectations, Isolation Forest) are fitted on the oldest $70\%$ of the rows and
scored on the newest $15\%$. A threshold is a quantile of the scores that keeps
the training prevalence, taken (P0) from the training scores, (P1) from the
validation scores, or (P2) from the scores of the two previous blocks of the test
period, without any label of that period. A stratified control, over five seeds,
is also matched to the prevalence of the chronological test.

On Bank Marketing the prevalence moves a lot: $5.8\%$ in training, $11.5\%$ in
validation and $39.3\%$ in the test period (Credit Card: $0.18\%$, $0.13\%$ and
$0.12\%$). The average precision under the chronological split is higher than
under the stratified one for every detector ($0.55$ against $0.17$ for the
Z-score, $0.42$ against $0.19$ for Isolation Forest), but the ROC-AUC of the
chronological split is between $0.48$ and $0.63$, close to chance. Matching the
control to the test prevalence brings its average precision to
$0.40$--$0.52$, so the gap, a median of $0.26$, falls to $0.06$: on this
dataset the apparent gain is a change in prevalence, not in detection. On Credit
Card the same matching reduces the median gap from $0.053$ to $0.031$, which is
less than half of it, so a smaller part of the gap is explained by prevalence
there.

The ranking of the detectors is not stable. The Spearman correlation between
the two orderings by average precision is $0.80$ on Bank Marketing and $0.40$ on
Credit Card, below the $0.8$ expected on both. The three thresholds do not
behave as I expected. On Bank Marketing the share of rows flagged moves closer to
the training prevalence from P0 to P1 to P2 (median error
$|\ln(\text{share}/\pi)|$ of $1.51$, $1.02$ and $0.66$), but on Credit Card P1
is no better than P0 ($0.78$ against $0.73$), so the ordering holds on one
dataset only. The reason is clearer in the decisions. On Bank Marketing, P0
flags $41\%$ of the test rows with the Z-score and $37\%$ with Isolation Forest,
because the later rows score higher than the training rows, which gives an $F_1$
of $0.51$ and $0.41$ by accident of a prevalence of $39\%$; the rolling
threshold targets a share of $5.8\%$, flags $1.7\%$ and $6.3\%$ of the rows and
drops the $F_1$ to $0.02$ and $0.11$. A label-free recalibration that keeps the
training prevalence cannot follow a prevalence that changes. On Credit Card the
opposite holds: no detector detects any of the $52$ frauds with P0 ($F_1 = 0$),
while the rolling threshold gives a recall of $0.12$ for Great Expectations and
$0.14$ for the Z-score, at a precision below $0.08$. The $F_1$ of P2 is at
least that of P0 for $5$ of the $8$ pairs, which satisfies the hypothesis, but
four of the five are Credit Card pairs where P0 is zero. Overall one hypothesis of
four holds. This study does not establish a rule for thresholds under shift: it
shows that a threshold fixed on the past can fail in both directions, that
recalibrating without labels needs an estimate of the prevalence, and that part
of what a chronological split changes is only the prevalence.

\subsection{LSTM autoencoder pilot}
\label{sec:lstm}
A last pilot asks whether the preceding rows of the transaction stream carry a
fraud signal that a row-level autoencoder cannot see. Credit Card has no
cardholder identifier, so the window is the global stream of the ten previous
rows, not a customer's history. The protocol was fixed beforehand
(\texttt{verification/lstm\_pilot.md}). The comparison uses the chronological
partitions of Section~\ref{sec:chrono}, the same $50{,}000$ training rows and a
CPU budget of at most $20$ epochs for both models; the architecture (window of
$10$ rows, $32$ units) was not tuned, and the validation rows served only for
early stopping on an unlabelled reconstruction loss.

As a check before reading the real data, a synthetic stream whose anomalies
break the continuity of an autoregressive signal was scored by both models: the
LSTM reaches an average precision of $0.875$ against $0.006$ for the
row-level autoencoder. On Credit Card, over five initialisation seeds, the LSTM
reaches an average precision of $0.037 \pm 0.006$ on the $52$ test frauds
against $0.024 \pm 0.010$ for the row-level autoencoder (ROC-AUC $0.926$
against $0.841$). The paired block-bootstrap interval of the difference excludes
zero for the main seed ($+0.021$, $[+0.005, +0.044]$) but not for the other
four, where the difference is positive for three seeds and negative for one.
This criterion was set in advance as an exploratory check, not a formal test.
The LSTM is comparable to Isolation Forest ($0.035$) and Great Expectations
($0.045$) on the same rows, not clearly better. None of the five trainings
converged within the budget: the validation loss was still decreasing at the
$20$th epoch. As for the other detectors, the threshold taken from training
scores flags almost none of the frauds in the later period (recall at most
$0.02$). The result is therefore an indication from a small, budget-limited
experiment on one dataset, not a ranking.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Rules and learned models are complementary, not competing.}
The per-type heatmap shows limited overlap between what the declarative
rules catch (missing values), what the statistical rules catch
(single-column outliers), and what the learned models add (implausible
combinations, recall $0.89$ for Isolation Forest against $\le 0.04$ for
the rules, and $1.00$ for DIF and $0.92$ for the VAE among the neural models). Broad coverage needs more than one family, not the single
best one on aggregate. This is a different conclusion from benchmarks
that publish a single ranking.

\paragraph{The operating point matters as much as the algorithm.} Setting
Isolation Forest's contamination to the data prevalence rather than a
fixed $0.10$ moves its fraud precision from $0.01$ to $0.28$ (the seed
of Table~\ref{tab:contamination}; $0.20$ on average over five seeds) and its
multivariate-outlier recall on the synthetic data from about $0.3$ to
$0.9$ (means over five seeds).
The same estimator looks weak or strong depending on one number that a
benchmark must choose deliberately.

\paragraph{Unsupervised detection works in one of the three regimes.}
The three example datasets bracket the method: on Cardio, where anomalies
are rare and distributionally distinct, Isolation Forest reaches
$F_1 = 0.62$ and PR-AUC $0.70$; on Credit Card Fraud, where the positive
class is a $0.17\%$ mixed-defect label, the best $F_1$ of the seventeen
detectors is $0.35$ (Deep SVDD, precision $0.26$) and HBOS reaches the best precision
($0.34$) at a recall of $0.32$; on Water Quality, where the ``anomaly''
is a $61\%$ majority, every method is at chance. The question is less
``rules or ML'' than ``does this dataset have rare, outlying anomalies at
all''.

\paragraph{The profile transfers in part, and the decision does not.}
The later studies test whether the synthetic profile is an artefact of one
generator. Two further schemas keep the ordering of the methods (rank
correlation $0.93$), so the competence of each family is not specific to the
customer table; but the multivariate and drift cells depend on how the columns
are scaled, so their values do not transfer. On the $42$ real tables the
structural rules still own their types while the multivariate gap shrinks, and
on the $100$ labelled problems the medians of PR-AUC of One-Class SVM ($0.240$),
Isolation Forest ($0.215$) and the Z-score ($0.203$) overlap, so the advice
``use a learned model'' is not supported there. The neural models add little on
aggregate: the best of them (DIF, $0.226$) stays below the best classical median
($0.240$), and the LSTM autoencoder recalls $0.25$ of the drifted rows against
$0.50$ for LOF; LUNAR is the exception on drift ($0.65$ on the customers), at the price of
flagging $41\%$ of the clean rows.
Last, a ranking that holds under a chronological split does not give a usable
threshold: on Bank Marketing the prevalence goes from $5.8\%$ to $39.3\%$,
matching the control to it removes most of the apparent gain ($0.26$ to $0.06$),
and a label-free rolling threshold cannot follow it. A practitioner should
therefore choose the family from the type of fault and monitor the prevalence
before trusting any alert rate.

\paragraph{A larger registry does not produce a winner.} The registry now holds three
rules, eight classical models and six neural ones. On the $100$ labelled problems every one
of the seventeen is first on at least two, and no method is first on more than $13$. The
column-wise detectors ECOD, COPOD and HBOS cost little, match Isolation Forest on the pool
($0.240$, $0.216$ and $0.237$ against $0.215$) and beat it on Credit Card, but they cannot
see an implausible combination of ordinary values; on the synthetic data they only do
because the injected combinations also push one value to a tail. The neural models help on
specific types (DIF and the VAE on multivariate outliers, LUNAR on drift) and
not on aggregate scores, and they cost more to fit and to tune. For a practitioner this
suggests a cheap column-wise model and Isolation Forest as the baseline, a reconstruction model
added when the faults are combinations, and a model such as LUNAR only when missing
drift costs more than false alarms. These are default settings, and a tuned neural model could do better.

\paragraph{Aggregate metrics on a mixed label are misleading.} On every
dataset the aggregate table compresses different behaviour into similar
$F_1$ and PR-AUC values. A method excellent on one anomaly type and blind
to the rest scores like a mediocre all-rounder. The controlled per-type
dataset, and the severity sweep that tests the sensitivity of that ranking,
are what make the difference measurable.

\paragraph{Pipeline integration changes the shortlist.} Latency and
scaling matter, but the timing in these runs is too variable to establish
a reliable speed ranking. The One-Class SVM and LOF were omitted from the
full fraud dataset because their computational costs need a separate
scaling study. I time only the detection stage (fit, predict and score)
inside the pipeline; extraction, transformation and loading are outside the
measurement, so these figures are not the latency of a whole ETL run.

\paragraph{Threats to validity of the results.} The synthetic dataset still encodes my
own assumptions about what each anomaly type looks like; the severity
sweep addresses the magnitude choice but not the shape of the
injections. The real-data pool answers part of this, but its anomalies are either
injected (the $42$ tables) or taken from a rare class (the $100$ labelled
problems), so none of them is a data-quality fault observed in a production
pipeline. The ML detectors carry a wide seed spread on the
small datasets (Cardio $F_1$ standard deviation up to $0.17$; Isolation
Forest on fraud $F_1 = 0.20 \pm 0.07$), so those numbers should be read
as ranges. The labels of the $64$ UCI problems come from a rule (the rarest
class), $19$ sources appear in both lists of the pool, and the datasets are
therefore not independent.

\paragraph{Limits of the verification.}
Verification has its own limits: complete line and branch coverage does not
establish that the scientific assertions detect every defect. The previous
archive was made on a working tree with uncommitted changes (revision
\texttt{841c3aa}). The archived runs were therefore replayed from a clean
commit (\texttt{93b6fe8}): every detection metric, curve and partition is
identical to the previous archive and only the timings changed, which is why
the two timing tables and the scaling figures above were updated. The new
records name that commit and mark the working tree as clean, but they were
produced on one machine and the timings do not transfer to another. Mutation
testing reaches the target on the pipeline modules and the scripts, but it
cannot show whether the injected defects and the tests cover every way an
experiment can go wrong. The complete real-data checks were performed with the three files available
locally. The Water
Quality file matches its named mirror, and the Cardio CSV can be reproduced
from the current ADBench archive. The Credit Card file has the size of the
current Kaggle version (3, March 2018), but I could not compare its content
hash with a fresh download. These
gaps leave some implementation errors capable of changing a published
result without being caught by the current gates.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

I built an ETL pipeline in which three rule-based detectors, eight classical
machine-learning detectors and six neural ones share one interface and are scored on
the same held-out path, over five seeds, with the operating point read
from the data. A controlled dataset labels six anomaly types separately,
and a severity sweep shows where the per-type ranking changes as the
injections are scaled. On aggregate metrics the detector families stay
close on the example and synthetic datasets, and on $100$ labelled problems of a pool of
$152$ open tables no detector is clearly ahead (Section~\ref{sec:labelled}). The per-type view is where they diverge:
declarative rules own missing values, statistical rules own single-column
outliers, tree-, boundary- and reconstruction-based models (Isolation Forest, the
One-Class SVM, DIF, the VAE) catch implausible combinations, and duplicates and temporal drift need
dedicated stages, LUNAR being the one learned model that helps on drift, at the price
of many false alarms. Across the three example datasets, unsupervised detection
pays off only on Cardio, where the anomalies are genuinely rare and
outlying. The choice of contamination, the precision-recall trade-off it
selects, and the pipeline cost further constrain which detector fits a
real flow.

On real tables the pattern is less clean than on the generator. With the six
types injected into $42$ tables, Great Expectations and the deduplication rule
still own their types, but the multivariate gap shrinks and the $z$-score
collapses for a reason that has to do with its training data, not its design.
On $100$ labelled problems no detector is clearly ahead among the seventeen, learning helps only
by a small margin, and the neural models do not beat the best classical one. Two further schemas keep the profile (rank correlation
$0.93$) but change how visible multivariate outliers and drift are, and an LSTM
autoencoder trained to convergence does not catch temporal drift better than
the row-level detectors. The chronological pilot and the study of decisions under temporal shift, on
Credit Card and Bank Marketing, point the same way: score rankings survive a
split by time better than a threshold fixed on the past. On Bank Marketing, where
the prevalence goes from $5.8\%$ to $39.3\%$, matching the control to it cuts
the apparent gain of the chronological split from $0.26$ to $0.06$ of PR-AUC,
and a label-free rolling threshold that keeps the training prevalence cannot
follow it; only one of the four hypotheses of that study holds.

The next steps are a check of the injected faults against faults observed in
real pipelines, and a decision-level study on more datasets with genuine time,
such as the Kyoto-2006+ traffic, whose data rights I have not verified. The open questions are listed
in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Verification design}
\label{app:design}
The experimental code is checked at three levels. A routine test suite
exercises numerical contracts on small, generated tables and measures
line and branch coverage across the pipeline and experiment scripts. Its
regression cases include partition disjointness by customer identity,
training-only preprocessing, the exclusion of labels from detector inputs,
score direction and index alignment, undefined binary metrics, and the
construction of each synthetic anomaly type. Separate command-line tests
run the four figure and table generators on temporary data. They check the
output schema without comparing images pixel by pixel or imposing a fixed
wall-clock duration.

A second, manually invoked level asks whether these checks can fail.
Mutation testing generates small source-level changes (a reversed
comparison, an altered constant, a modified message) and counts how many
make the routine suite fail. I apply it to the modules that compute a
result and to the experiment scripts. In addition, twenty-three deliberate
scientific defects are applied one at a time to a temporary copy of the
code, and a named test must fail on each: a scaler refitted on the rows
being scored, the label passed to the detector as a feature, a contamination
threshold read from the test labels, a training row copied into the test
partition, an inverted score orientation, ranking metrics computed from the
binary flags, a seed aggregate that keeps only the first seed, a detector
missing from the results table, and a few others.

A third level uses the locally available full datasets. A separate command
compares, for each of the three research CSV files, the file checksum, the raw
and post-deduplication row counts, the number of anomalies and the
positive-class value with an archived manifest. An absent file is reported as
not run, and the command as a whole passes only if all three files pass. The
scripts record the dataset fingerprint, software versions, random seeds,
split policy and output names alongside their results. For each real-data
seed, an archive stores the positions of the train, validation and test rows
after deduplication; a capped fit also records the selected training
positions. Archived tables and figures are checked against their manifest,
and the six numerical tables in this manuscript are compared with the
archived CSV values at the precision shown. This establishes traceability
between the reported values and the recorded experiment; it does not
establish that another machine will reproduce the same timings.

A fourth part turns these checks into a release verdict. Each profile (routine
suite, mutation campaign, fault injection, real-data check, coverage of the
checking scripts themselves, static typing, and a controlled reproduction)
writes a small evidence record tied to the source revision, to a clean working
tree, to a fingerprint of the code that computes results (the pipeline and the
experiment scripts, not the checking scripts) and to the software versions. A
release script recomputes every status from the raw results instead of reading
it from the record, requires that all profiles describe the same revision and
compatible environments, and returns \emph{certified}, \emph{incomplete} or
\emph{rejected}; figures quoted in this manuscript are accepted only if the run
they come from is not older than a change to the code. The real-data check can
also run on a hosted Linux machine: it downloads the three public files,
compares each SHA-256 value with the archived manifest before using it, and
refuses any other file. The controlled reproduction replays the cardiotocography
benchmark against the archive, twice in the same environment and once in an
environment rebuilt from a recorded, hash-pinned lock; on another operating
system the metrics agree statistically (largest difference $6.5\times10^{-5}$,
identical partitions) rather than bit for bit. Result files are written to a
temporary name and renamed only when complete, so an interrupted run cannot leave
a truncated table or figure. The verdict states where the evidence was produced
and never that it was independently attested: the author wrote the code and
launched every run.

I treat a failed or missing check as a limitation of the evidence, not as
a successful experiment. Complete line and branch coverage and a mutation
score of at least $95\%$ over the pipeline and the scripts are the targets, and the
measured values, with what they leave out, are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:outcomes}. The
chronological split and the window-based drift monitor have a separate
protocol: their alert and delay measures are not pooled with the row-level
metrics from the stratified benchmark.
